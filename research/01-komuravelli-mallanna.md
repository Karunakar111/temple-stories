# Episode 1 research: Komuravelli Mallanna

Compiled 2026-11-22. Sources listed at the bottom; every claim below is tagged with
how confident we should be on screen.

**Confidence key**
- **[A] Solid** — stated by the state government, mainstream press, or multiple
  independent sources that agree.
- **[B] Reasonable** — widely repeated in reference and regional sources, consistent
  with scholarship, but not independently verified.
- **[C] Variant** — one version of an oral story that genuinely differs between tellers.
  Say "one telling goes…" on screen, never "this is what happened".
- **[?] Needs a human check** — ask the temple, an Oggu performer, or a Telugu source.

---

## 1. The deity

**[A]** Mallanna (also Mallikarjuna Swamy) is a form of Shiva, worshipped at a hill temple
in **Komuravelli village, Siddipet district, Telangana**. The temple is run by the
Telangana Endowments Department.

**[A]** He belongs to a single Deccan deity-family that crosses three language regions:

| Region | Name |
|---|---|
| Telangana / Andhra | **Mallanna** / Mallikarjuna |
| Karnataka | **Mailara** / Mailaralinga |
| Maharashtra | **Khandoba** / Malhari Martand |

This is the most interesting fact available for Episode 1 and it is well established.
He is a **pastoral god** — a god of shepherds and herders before he was a temple god.

**[B]** The idol is clay, and is commonly described as around 500 years old. Reference
sources repeat this; I found no inscription or archaeological citation behind it.
**On screen: "said to be about five centuries old", not "is 500 years old".**

**[A]** Shared iconography across the three regions: a **horse**, a **dog**, a **sword**,
and above all **turmeric** — *bhandara* in Marathi, *bandaram* in Telugu. Turmeric is
thrown, smeared and offered in handfuls; it is the single most recognisable visual of
this cult, and it is a gift to an animator.

## 2. The story

**[A] The crucial fact, and the spine of the episode: there is no written scripture for
Mallanna.** His story survives because it is *sung* — in **Oggu Katha**, the ballad
tradition of the Kuruma and Golla (Yadava) communities. Wikipedia states plainly that
"there is no written script for the Lord Mallanna story."

Compare: the Maharashtra cousin Khandoba *does* have a Sanskrit text, the *Malhari
Mahatmya*, which scholars (R. C. Dhere, G. D. Sontheimer) date to roughly **1460–1510 CE**
**[B]**. So the same god has a manuscript in one region and only a song in another.

**That contrast is the episode.** A god whose story was never written down, kept alive for
centuries by singers who inherited it from their fathers.

### 2a. The marriage story — the core Oggu Katha narrative **[C]**

Mallanna has **two wives, from two different communities** — this is the point of the
story, not a detail:

- **Ketamma** (Golla Ketamma) — from the **Golla / Yadava** herding community
- **Medalamma** — described as a princess of the **Linga Balija** community

**[C]** In the telling that circulates most: Medalamma's brothers set Mallanna a
condition before they will allow the marriage — he must obtain *Bandari* (the sacred
turmeric, also called *Pasupu Kommulu*). He reaches the hidden place, takes the Bandari,
and escapes **disguised as a black dog**, pursued by two figures named **Haseen and
Hussain**. The goddess **Ganga** loses her right hand shielding him. He returns to
Komuravelli on a **flying horse**, gives a share of the Bandari to **Yellamma**, and
marries Medalamma.

Treat every beat of this as one telling. Different Oggu troupes tell it differently, and
that is normal and not an error in the tradition.

**[B]** The parallel Khandoba legend: two demons, **Mani and Malla**, win invincibility
from Brahma; the god defeats them; the defeated **Mani gives the god his white horse**.
The god takes the name *Mallari* — "enemy of Malla" — from the demon he beat. This is
the written version, and it is a clean way to show how the same god is remembered
differently on either side of a language border.

### 2b. The temple's own origin story **[B/?]**

One account says a man named **Komaranna**, of the Yadava community, performed severe
penance and installed the idol on the hill, and that the village name follows from his.
Repeated in several travel and reference write-ups; I could not trace it to the temple
or the Endowments Department. **[?] Verify with the temple before stating it.**

## 3. Oggu Katha — the storytellers

**[A]** A Telangana ballad form in which the **Oggus**, hereditary priests of the Golla
and Kuruma communities, travel from place to place singing the stories of their gods —
**Mallanna, Beerappa and Yellamma**.

**[A]** Performance shape:
- **Four to six performers**: a chief narrator, a second narrator who answers him, and
  instrumentalists.
- **Instruments**: *jaggu* (a damaru-type drum), *dolu*, *thalam* (brass cymbals), and in
  fuller troupes *rana bheri* (a large drum), *kanjira* and *napheera* (a wind instrument).
- **The narrators switch into the characters** — it is sung narration that turns into
  acted dialogue. That shift is what makes the form live.

**[A]** The chief narrator's costume is specific and worth animating accurately: a wide
pant tied at the knee, a coloured shirt, a coloured head cloth, and heavy ornament —
including *gavvala darshanam*, a chain of seven cowrie shells, and *ambarala golusu*, a
garland carrying Mallanna's portrait.

**[A]** Named masters of the form, useful if the episode credits the living tradition:
**Chukka Sattaiah**, **Midde Ramulu**, **Kathala Mallaiah**.

**[?]** Chukka Sattaiah is widely described as the best-known Oggu Katha performer.
Confirm whether he is living before referring to him in the present tense.

## 4. The temple and its year

**[A] Dual priesthood** — the clearest social fact about this temple, and unusual:

| Who | Where | What |
|---|---|---|
| **Linga Balija / Veerashaiva** priests | inside the sanctum | daily puja, wear the *ishtalinga*, maintain the clay idol |
| **Oggu Pujaris** (Kuruma and Yadava) | outside, in the open | Oggu Katha, and the *Patnam* floor drawings |

Two traditions, two communities, one temple — mapped onto the god's two wives. This is
the kind of thing a short animated film can show in ten seconds and an essay takes a page
to explain.

**[A] Patnam**: a ritual floor drawing (a rangoli, in turmeric and coloured powders) made
by the Oggu Pujaris before the deity and in the verandah. The larger **Pedda Patnam**
falls at **Maha Shivaratri**.

**[A] The jatara runs from Sankranti to Ugadi**, and the **Sundays** in between are the
days — Mallanna is a Sunday god. Devotees bring **bonams** in new pots. **Agni Gundaalu**
(the fire pit) is held on the **Sunday before Ugadi**.

**[A] The 2026 season, as actually held** (from *The Hans India*, and useful as a model for
what 2027 will look like):
- Kalyanam: **14 December 2025, 10:45 AM**
- Jatara: **18 January – 16 March 2026**, ten weeks
- Jatara Sundays: Jan 18, 25; Feb 1, 8, 15, 22; Mar 1, 8, 15
- Mahashivaratri: **15 February 2026**
- Oggu Katha performed in the evenings through the season
- State coordination across police, RTC, health, electricity, water and R&B departments

**[A]** The state is investing in the temple as a tourism destination: ₹36.18 crore from
the Endowments Department plus ₹9.776 crore from a special development fund.
**[A]** A railway station on the Kothapalli–Manoharabad line opened in 2026 to serve it.

### Publishing window — act on this

**The 2026 jatara is over.** The next one begins around **mid-January 2027**: Sankranti is
**14 January 2027**, and if the pattern holds the jatara opens on the first Sunday after,

## 5. Handle with care

**Communities are not background detail.** Golla/Yadava, Kuruma, and Linga Balija are
named, living communities whose claim on this god is the story. Name them accurately and
respectfully, never generically as "tribals" or "villagers", and never imply hierarchy
between the sanctum priests and the Oggu priests.

**Haseen and Hussain, and Muslim veneration.** **[A]** In Maharashtra, Muslims venerate
Khandoba as **Mallu Khan** or **Malluka Pathan**, and a Muslim family traditionally cares
for the god's horses at Jejuri. The Telugu telling carries Haseen and Hussain as pursuers.
This Hindu–Muslim weave is genuine, old, and documented. **Recommendation for Episode 1:
leave it out.** It deserves its own episode with proper sourcing and care; dropped into a
90-second film it will be flattened or misread. Do not sanitise it elsewhere — just don't
rush it here.

**Animal sacrifice. [?]** Sacrifice is historically associated with parts of the
Mailara/Mallanna complex. I have not verified current practice at Komuravelli. **Do not
depict it, and do not mention it, without checking.**

**"Incarnation of Shiva" vs "folk god absorbed into Shiva".** Devotees and the state say
*incarnation of Shiva*. Scholars describe an independent pastoral deity later identified
with Shiva. Both are true in their own register. Use the devotee's language on screen —
this is a channel for devotees — and let the pastoral origin show through the imagery
(sheep, herders, turmeric, the open Deccan) rather than through a correcting voiceover.

## 8. The patnam is not decoration. It is the story. **[A]**

From *Sakshi* (major Telugu daily) and the Telangana folklore literature:

> **"జానపద రూపాల్లోని ఒగ్గు పూజారులు కథను చెబుతూ పట్నం వేస్తారు"**
> *The Oggu priests lay the patnam **while telling the story**.*

The drawing and the telling are **one act**. The Oggu priest narrates Mallanna's story and
draws it into the ground at the same time. The patnam is the story in powder.

**[A] Made of five natural colours:** turmeric (yellow), vermillion (red), rose petals
(pink), dried green-leaf powder (green), rice flour (white).

**[A] Pedda Patnam** at Maha Shivaratri: laid near the temple well, roughly **50 yards
across, 41 rows**.

**[A] Ritual objects** set before it: the **oragommu** (Mallanna's single-pronged trident)
and the **damaru**.

### What the patnam actually looks like **[A] — photograph**

`design/references/patnam-pedda-patam-photo.jpg` — an overhead photograph of the
Komuravelli **pedda patam**, captioned Shivaratri 2023. This is now the only visual
authority we use for its geometry. What is *visible* in it, and nothing more:

- One **square**, laid flat on the courtyard, the crowd standing packed on all four sides.
- Built from **many thin concentric square borders** — straight lines throughout, corners
  mitred so a clean diagonal seam runs out to each corner. The count is consistent with
  the sourced **41 rows** above.
- Each row is a narrow stripe of powder, finely stippled: **white, golden yellow, orange,
  deep brick red**, repeating. The overall read is **yellow and white, not red**.
- Towards the middle the bands widen and one carries a ring of **evenly spaced pale round
  dabs** running right around the square.
- At the exact centre, a **small square divided into four panels**, each holding one small
  pale mark. The photograph does not resolve what those marks are, so **we do not say**.
- The courtyard around it is stone **stained and streaked turmeric-yellow**, with loose
  heaps of turmeric at the edge.

**No circle anywhere. No mandala, no lotus rosette, no animal, no figure.**

> **Correction.** Our first shot-3 artwork drew the patnam as a circular floral mandala
> with a horse and a dog worked into it, and `docs/PILOT_REPORT.md` justified the horse as
> "the drawing depicts the story being sung." That was an inference, not a source. §8 says
> the priest *lays the patnam while telling the story* — it does not say the story is drawn
> **figuratively**. The photograph shows it is not. Claim withdrawn.

### And then the powder goes into the fields **[B]**

After the puja, the god's sacred animals cross the patnam, and devotees gather up the
scattered powder:

> **"ఈ ముగ్గును పొలాల్లో చల్లితే పంటలు బాగా పండుతాయని"**
> *If this muggu is scattered in the fields, the crops will grow well.*

**One source (Sakshi), so [B] — but if it holds, it is the whole episode.** The story is
sung → drawn into the ground → walked across → carried home → scattered on farmland →
becomes the harvest. A story that turns into food.

**[?] Karunakar must confirm this.** It is the kind of thing a Telangana family knows
first-hand, and it cannot go on screen as fact on one newspaper feature alone.

## 9. Two weddings a year, one for each tradition **[A]**

The god is married **twice annually, by two different rites**:
- **Veerashaiva Agama** tradition — last Sunday of Margashira
- **Yadu (Golla) tradition** — at Maha Shivaratri

The dual priesthood from §4 is not symbolic. Each community marries the god in its own way.

## 10. No Kuruma wedding happens until this story is told **[A]**

From the Telugu folklore text (*Telugu vaari jaanapada kalaarupaalu*, Wikisource):

> **"మల్లన్న కథను కురుమల వివాహ సందర్భాలలో చెపుతూ వుంటారు"**
> *The Mallanna story is told at Kuruma wedding occasions* — and the marriage is
> conducted **only after the story has been told**.

The Oggu performers are hereditary **purohits**, with territorial boundaries (*హద్దులు*);
crossing another's territory risks excommunication.

So the story is not entertainment and not heritage. It is **load-bearing**: ordinary
families cannot get married until it has been sung.

## 11. Mallanna is born from Shiva's sweat **[B]**

Kuruma origin tradition: **Beerappa** came from Shiva's *first* sweat (**తొలి చెమట**),
**Mallanna** from the *second* (**మలి చెమట**). Specific, strange, and not the standard
"incarnation of Shiva" line. One folklore source — [B].

## 12. "Oggu" means Shiva's drum **[A]**

> **"శైవ సంప్రదాయంలో ఒక వర్గం వారు శివుని డమరుకాన్ని ఒగ్గు అంటారని"**
> *In Shaiva tradition one group calls Shiva's damaru an "oggu."*

The art form is named after the drum in Shiva's hand. Performers invoke **Ganga Devi**
before beginning.

## 13. Other additions **[A]**

- Families lay **patnams at home**, every one to three years, to fulfil vows — relatives
  invited, a household festival.
- Devotees travel from **Odisha, Chhattisgarh and Karnataka**. Pan-regional, not local.
- *Sakshi* gives the jatara as **19 January – 24 March**; *The Hans India* gave
  18 January – 16 March 2026. **[?] Dates vary by year and by source — never state a
  future date without the temple's own announcement.**

## 16. The idol is termite-mound clay — పుట్టమట్టి **[B]**

> *"సుమారు ఐదు వందల సంవత్సరాల క్రితమే దట్టమైన అడవుల మధ్య ఈ మృత్తికా విగ్రహాన్ని"*
> *About five hundred years ago, this clay idol, in the middle of dense forest.*

Not just "clay" — **పుట్టమట్టి, soil from a termite mound.** This matters: Sontheimer
independently records that Khandoba across the Deccan is associated with **termite
mounds**. Local tradition and outside scholarship arrive at the same thing. Under the
sthala-puranam frame this can be told as it is told.

## 18. The wives — and a third one I had missed

**Golla Ketamma [A]** — daughter of the **Yadava / Golla** community.

**Balija Medalamma [A]** — daughter of the **Linga Balija** community. Married after
Mallanna met **conditions set by her brothers**:
> *"మేడాలమ్మను ఆమె సోదరులు పెట్టిన షరతులకు అంగీకరించి మల్లన్న వివాహం చేసుకున్నాడు"*

**[B] And she is said to be an avatar of Bhramaramba Devi** — the goddess of Srisailam.
Which quietly ties Komuravelli's Mallikarjuna to Srisailam's Mallikarjuna. A devotee would
feel that connection.

**Bapana Ratnangi [C] — a Brahmin wife, in the Kuruma telling only.** And the detail that
makes it matter: **the Oggu Pujaris claim descent from her line.** Their right to be the
god's priests is written into the god's marriage. Only one source — but as a *story*,
under the sthala-puranam frame, it is the best single thing found in three passes.

## 19. The Oggu priests, concretely **[A]**

- **Hereditary surnames**: Majjiga, **Bandari**, Are, Sera, Marate.
- **Two kinds**: **Jaggollu** (temple-based, play the *jaggu*) and **Kurmollu**
  (travelling performers).
- **Their role in a Golla wedding** begins **three days before**: *maila polu* (the bride's
  ritual preparation), the drum procession, then **Nagavelli** at the wedding itself —
  Surya Namaskaram and songs of **Mallanna's own marriage** — and they return **eight days
  later** for *gadapa kadagatam*, washing the threshold.

So when the texts say no Golla/Kuruma wedding happens until the Mallanna story is told,
this is the shape of it. A family's wedding is built around the god's wedding.

**[A]** Mallanna Panduga is given here as **Kartika Amavasya → Ugadi** — longer than the
Sankranti→Ugadi window in §4. **[?] Both are probably true at different scales.**

## 21. Where the full story actually is — and it is not on the web

Three passes, and no source on the open web carries the full narrative. That is consistent
rather than disappointing: **the story has no written text.** To get it properly:

1. **Recorded Oggu Katha performances** — the primary source, and they exist on video.
2. **Folklore scholarship** — Shodhganga theses and Telugu folklore collections that

# Fourth pass — the Bandari story, in full (2026-11-22)

Looking for a *complete* narrative after Karunakar's reference reel. Found it. The full
spine, from Telugu sources:

1. Mallanna wants to marry **Medalamma**. Her **brothers set a condition**: bring the
   **bandari** — **పసుపు కొమ్ములు**, turmeric roots or "horns".
2. The bandari is held by **Haseen and Hussain**, **at Mecca Masjid**.
3. The gods give Mallanna a **flying horse**. He flies there.
4. He takes service as a **gardener**, under the assumed name **Malku Malloji**, and
   waits — many days, some tellings say years — until he is completely trusted.
5. The bandari is guarded by a **five-headed serpent**.
6. He takes it. He is seen. He escapes **in the form of a black dog**, pursued.
7. **Ganga loses her right hand** shielding him.
8. He returns on the flying horse, gives a share to **Yellamma**, and marries Medalamma.
9. **That bandari is the turmeric thrown by the fistful at the jatara.**

## This is a complete story — and it is sensitive

Dramatically it is excellent: a goal, an impossible condition, a long patient deception, a
guarded prize, a theft, a chase, a sacrifice, a return, and a resolution into a ritual the
viewer has performed with their own hands. Exactly the shape of the Chhappan Bhog reel.

**And it is a Hindu god infiltrating a mosque in Mecca under an assumed name to take a
sacred object, pursued by two Muslim figures.**

That is genuinely how the tradition tells it. Note also that the assumed name **Malku
Malloji** echoes **Mallu Khan / Malluka Pathan**, the names by which Muslims venerate this
same god in Maharashtra (§5) — the entanglement runs in both directions, and the story is
a product of a shared Deccan world, not a hostile one.

But on a public channel in 2026, told cold to a new audience with no established context,
it can be read as communal content regardless of intent — and amplified as such by people
who want it to be.

### Options for Episode 1

| | Keeps | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Tell it in full** | Everything, including Mecca and the two names | Materially different risk category from the reference reel. Could define the channel's audience on day one, and poison the crowdsourced-comments plan |
| **B. Tell it with the destination unnamed** | Quest, flying horse, the gardener's disguise and long wait, the five-headed serpent, the theft, the black dog, the chase, Ganga's hand, the turmeric | Loses the most distinctive element; a knowing viewer may note the omission |
| **C. A different story for Episode 1** | Safety | No other complete narrative is sourced yet |

**Recommendation: B for Episode 1.** Every dramatic beat survives — nothing invented,
nothing contradicted, the ending still lands on the turmeric in the viewer's hand. The
fuller telling deserves its own episode later, with room to frame the syncretism properly,
once the channel has an audience that knows its register. **Karunakar's call, not mine.**
