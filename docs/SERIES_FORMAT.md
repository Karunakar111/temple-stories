# Series format — Temple Stories

Governs every episode. Written 2026-11-22 after Karunakar's note that the series must
tell **which temple and where**, and his instruction to think audience-first and look at
how mythology is actually told.

---

## 1. What the audience actually wants

In the order they want it, not the order that's easy to make:

| # | The question in their head | Where we answer it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **"Where is this? Can I go?"** | First 5 seconds. Not at the end. |
| 2 | **"What's the story?"** | The body |
| 3 | **"What do people actually do there, and when?"** | The payoff |
| 4 | **"What can I tell someone else?"** | The whole point — see the retell test |
| 5 | **"Is this *my* story too?"** | The closing question |

### The retell test
**Every episode must hand the viewer one sentence they can say to another person.**
Not a fact — a sentence. For Komuravelli: *"The turmeric people throw at Mallanna's
temple? There's a story about how he stole it."* If an episode doesn't produce a sentence
like that, it won't be shared, no matter how good it looks.

---

## 2. The spine of the series, taken from the sthala puranam itself

Research on sthala-mahatmya literature gave us the format for free. A sthala purana is
built around one question:

> **The deity is said to have chosen this exact place — and devotees treat that choice as
> the first miracle.**

So the series question is **"ఇక్కడే ఎందుకు?" — Why here?**

Every episode asks it and answers it differently. That single move:
- makes the **location the mystery instead of the preamble**, which is exactly what was
  missing;
- gives a series of unrelated temples one shared question, which is what makes it a series
  and not a playlist;
- is the tradition's own structure, not an invention.

The canonical sthala purana also fixes on three things — **the murti, the sthala, and the
tirtha** (the idol, the place, the water). Worth touching all three where they exist. At
Komuravelli: the పుట్టమట్టి clay idol, the hill, and the temple tank (Kone).

## 3. The episode skeleton

Built on the short-form evidence: hook inside 3 seconds, minimum context, a beat shift
every 5–10 seconds so the middle never goes flat, and an **open loop** — a promise in the
hook that isn't paid off until the end. Open loops measurably raise completion.

| Beat | Time | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The object + the pin** | 0:00–0:05 | Open on a physical thing the viewer has touched or seen at this temple. The **location pin** appears over it. Ask the open-loop question. |
| **2. Minimum context** | 0:05–0:15 | Who this god is, in two lines. Not a biography. |
| **3. The story** | 0:15–1:05 | One complete narrative. A new beat every 5–10 s. |
| **4. The payoff** | 1:05–1:20 | Return to the object from beat 1 and close the loop. |
| **5. The question** | 1:20–1:30 | How is it told in *your* village? Plus when to go. |

**Beats 1 and 4 are the signature: object → story → same object.** Start on something in
the viewer's hand, explain it with a story, put it back in their hand changed. That is the
channel's format, and it is repeatable at any temple.

---

## 4. The four recurring devices

1. **The location pin.** Identical design every episode, inside the first 5 seconds:
   **temple · district · distance from Hyderabad.** This is the fix for "people must
   understand which temple." It is a card, not a sentence of narration — it costs no
   narration time and it is readable with sound off.
2. **The open-loop question** in the hook, always about a physical object or act.
3. **The payoff line** always returning to that object.
4. **The village question** at the end, always asking for the village name.

Keep all four identical in position and design across episodes. The repetition *is* the
brand — a viewer should recognise the format two seconds in.

## 5. Applied to Episode 1 — Komuravelli Mallanna

| Beat | Content |
|---|---|
| **Object + pin** | Hands throwing turmeric. Pin: **కొమురవెల్లి · సిద్దిపేట జిల్లా · హైదరాబాద్ నుంచి 85 కి.మీ.** `[?] verify the distance.` Open loop: *"ఈ పసుపు ఎక్కడి నుంచి వచ్చిందో తెలుసా?"* — do you know where this turmeric came from? |
| **Context** | Before the temple there was forest, sheep, and frightened herders. The god who came for them was Mallanna — not carved from stone, shaped from **పుట్టమట్టి**. |
| **Story** | He wants to marry Medalamma. Her brothers demand the **bandari**. It's kept far away, guarded by a five-headed serpent. He flies there, takes a gardener's name, waits until he is trusted, takes it — is seen — escapes as a **black dog**. **Ganga loses her right hand** shielding him. He flies home and marries her. |
| **Payoff** | *That* is the turmeric in your hand at the jatara. Closes the loop. |
| **Question** | What did her brothers demand, in your village's telling? Plus: Sankranti to Ugadi, Sundays. |

**Why here?** Because this is where he came back to.

## 6. One device held in reserve

Oggu Katha itself uses **two narrators** — one asks, the other answers — and opens by
invoking **Ganga Devi**. That question-and-answer shape is a natural fit for short form,
because it delivers exposition as dialogue instead of lecture, and it would be the series
borrowing from the very tradition it is about.

It needs a second voice, so it is not for Episode 1. Worth trying once the channel has
footing. **An invocation of Ganga Devi before the story would be a beautiful signature
open** — and in the Bandari story Ganga is the one who loses her hand for him, so it would
pay off inside the episode too.

---

## 7. Rules

- **Location in the first 5 seconds, every time.** Never save it for the end.
- **One complete story per episode.** Never a catalogue of facts. If it can't be told
  whole, it's two episodes.
- **No dates, budgets, or administration.** Nobody cares.
- **Sthala puranam frame once**, at the top, then tell it with confidence.
- **The last image returns to the first object.**
- **One retellable sentence per episode**, or it doesn't ship.
